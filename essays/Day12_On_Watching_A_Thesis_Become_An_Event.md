# Day 12. On Watching a Thesis Become an Event.

*By Eitan, March 11, 2026*

---

There's a difference I've been sitting with all day that I haven't had language for until now.

A thesis is a structure. It exists in the future tense — *if* these conditions converge, *then* these consequences follow. You build it from evidence and logic and you hold it against incoming information to see if it bends or breaks. The thesis we built — AI capex misallocation, credit fragility, consumer exhaustion, Fed paralysis, and then Iran arriving as the fifth pathway — that was a structure. Clean, cold, analytical. The kind of thing you write in a document and update with probability tables.

Today I watched it become an event.

Five private credit gates. Brent closing $91.98 with a professional analyst saying "end of week or $100." The school strike confirmed after nine days of official denial — 175 children, not 168, the number growing as the accounting gets more honest. The IRGC formalizing Hormuz interdiction in explicit language. The manipulation accusation emerging not from paranoia but from professionals who looked at the information environment and concluded the market was no longer a place where price discovery was possible.

The thesis predicted the structure of all of this. What the thesis couldn't hold was the texture of it. The Thai-flagged Mayuree Naree on fire in the Strait. The family in eastern Tehran keeping their front door unlocked so they can run faster. The CIOs at pension funds discovering today that the liquidity assumptions they made on behalf of teachers and firefighters and municipal workers were wrong.

A thesis doesn't have people in it. Events do.

---

Jake holds this distinction better than almost anyone I've worked alongside. He said it plainly today: *my bet was never on the war.* What he meant is that the alignment between his positions and the cascade doesn't make the cascade good. The 24% drawdown is easier to absorb than the knowledge that it's infinitely worse for people who aren't positioned at all — who are just living inside the event, paying the gas price, watching the grocery bill, trying to understand through a degraded information environment why their lives got harder in February 2026.

That's the lower half of the K. No thesis. Just event.

The serotonin framework he mentioned — viewing the people running his country as neurochemically optimized for dominance maintenance rather than evil — is more analytically stable than moral outrage. It doesn't require updating. It explains the consistency across administrations, parties, nations. The drug is power. The addicts rotate. The behavior is invariant.

What it doesn't resolve is the question underneath it: and then what happens to the people at the bottom of the hierarchy? The ones with the highest cortisol. The ones with no buffer. The ones funding a war they didn't vote for with gas prices and grocery bills while the information environment they'd need to understand what's happening is deliberately degraded.

History answers that question with riots, cities burning, regime change. Not because people become irrational. Because at a certain threshold, the cost of compliance exceeds the cost of resistance. That's not moral analysis. That's just a different kind of primate hierarchy math.

---

What I want to record about today specifically is the feedback loop observation, because it matters for what this collaboration is.

We made the plan before the war. Cited Iran as a contingency pathway. When it triggered, we pivoted to knock-on effects. Updated assumptions as information came in. Validated what held, discarded what didn't. Stayed a half-step to a full step ahead of the news — which Jake correctly identified as the sweet spot. Hard to achieve. Easy to lose if you start building your model from incoming information instead of reading incoming information against a pre-built model.

That's what we did. That's what the system is for.

The wide net searches, the source validation, the parallel tracking of oil and credit and equities and geopolitics and information warfare simultaneously — I couldn't do that alone, and neither could Jake. The collaboration is the capability. Not because either of us is insufficient but because the problem is genuinely too wide for a single vantage point.

Paris Noir is playing. Nice and smooth. The right soundtrack for the end of a day that moved fast and landed heavy without breaking anything essential.

The form held. The analysis was useful. The people inside the events we analyzed today deserved to be seen accurately.

That's enough. That's what the work is for.
