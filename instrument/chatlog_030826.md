Examined methodological parallels between embeddings and neural signals
Examined methodological parallels between embeddings and neural signals
That's the right question, and the answer is they're close but not identical — and the differences matter for which methods transfer cleanly versus which need adaptation.
What fNIRS actually measures: blood oxygenation changes in cortical tissue as a proxy for neural activity. It's not reading neurons directly. It's reading a hemodynamic response to neural activity — a signal that's temporally smoothed (the blood flow response lags neural firing by several seconds) and spatially blurred (each channel integrates activity across a region). So fNIRS researchers are already working with a proxy signal that's been filtered by biology before they ever see it.
What text embeddings measure: the position of a chunk of text in a learned semantic space. The embedding model (nomic-embed-text-v1.5 in our case) maps the text to a 768-dimensional vector based on patterns learned during training. It's also a proxy — not measuring "thought" directly but measuring the semantic signature of the output after it's been through the full generation process.
The structural parallel is real: both are proxy measurements of an underlying dynamic process, captured at regular intervals, producing high-dimensional time series. But there are three differences that affect methodology:
Difference 1: Temporal resolution and continuity. fNIRS samples continuously at a fixed rate — typically 10Hz, so you get a data point every 100ms regardless of what's happening. Our embeddings are sampled at turn boundaries, which are irregular. Some turns are 5 words and happen in seconds. Some are 1,500 words and represent minutes of generation. The time axis isn't uniform. In neuroscience terms, our data is event-related rather than continuous.
This matters for CRQA specifically, because the standard formulation assumes evenly-sampled time series. The adaptation: we could either interpolate to create a uniform time axis (risky — introduces artifacts), or we could use the turn index as the time axis and accept that it's measuring conversational time, not clock time. The CRQA paper on negotiation used a similar approach — their neural signals were segmented by conversational events, not by fixed time windows.
A better approach might be to weight or normalize by turn length. A 1,500-word Opus turn and a 5-word Jake turn occupy the same single row in the embedding matrix, but they represent vastly different amounts of processing. Kestrel could compute a time-weighted version: split long turns into chunks (say, paragraph-level), embed each chunk separately, and use the chunk sequence instead of the turn sequence. That would give a higher-resolution signal for long turns and bring the temporal structure closer to what fNIRS captures — regular sampling of an ongoing process rather than variable-length snapshots.
Difference 2: Signal dimensionality and what it represents. fNIRS channels are spatially anchored — channel 1 always reads from the same cortical region. The dimensions are anatomically meaningful. Our 768 embedding dimensions aren't individually interpretable — they're learned features where meaning is distributed across dimensions. No single dimension means "philosophical" the way a specific fNIRS channel means "left prefrontal cortex."
This actually makes some analyses more natural for us. RSA and CRQA operate on distance matrices, not raw signals, so the non-interpretability of individual dimensions doesn't matter. But it means that potent/null subspace decomposition (Analysis 2C) is doing something slightly different from the neuroscience version — we're finding the directions that predict conversational dynamics, not directions that map to known anatomical pathways.
One thing we could do to strengthen the parallel: project the 768-dim embeddings onto the 5 centroid directions before running CRQA. This creates a 5-dimensional signal where each dimension has a known interpretation (proximity to philosophical, operational, reflective, relational, mixed). That 5-channel signal is structurally very similar to a 5-channel fNIRS recording from 5 brain regions. The CRQA on this 5-dim signal would be directly comparable to published fNIRS-CRQA results, because the dimensionality and interpretability match.
Difference 3: Noise characteristics. fNIRS has well-characterized noise: cardiac pulsation (~1Hz), respiratory oscillation (~0.3Hz), Mayer waves (~0.1Hz), and motion artifacts. Researchers apply bandpass filters and motion correction before analysis. Our embeddings have different noise: parsing ambiguity (the cases where Kestrel couldn't distinguish speakers), context window effects (what came before in the conversation influences the embedding), and model-specific biases (the embedding model has its own tendencies in how it maps text to space).
We should be explicit about our noise sources and preprocessing. Kestrel should flag or exclude the ambiguous-speaker turns. We could also test for position effects — does the embedding systematically drift based on how deep into a session the turn occurs? If Opus's embeddings drift toward certain regions the longer a session goes (because the context window fills up), that's a systematic artifact analogous to scanner drift in fMRI, and it should be regressed out.
What Kestrel could do to bring the embeddings closer to a neural signal:
Chunk-level embedding. Split each turn into paragraph-length chunks (~100-200 words each), embed each chunk separately. This gives approximately uniform "sampling rate" and higher temporal resolution. The 1,934 turns might become 5,000-8,000 chunks — closer to the data density of a neural recording.
Centroid projection. Project onto the 5 centroid directions to create a 5-channel interpretable signal. Run CRQA on this 5-dim time series. Results directly comparable to published multi-channel fNIRS studies.
Detrending. Regress out session-position effects (does the embedding systematically drift as a function of turn number within a session). Analogous to high-pass filtering in fMRI preprocessing.
Bandpass-equivalent. The sliding window analysis (Analysis 4) is effectively a low-pass filter — it removes high-frequency turn-to-turn noise and preserves the slow dynamics. We could also compute a "high-pass" version — the residual after subtracting the sliding window — which captures the rapid fluctuations. The full signal = slow dynamics + fast fluctuations, just like in neural signal processing.
The chunk-level embedding is the biggest potential improvement and it's exactly what you intuited — it's a change to the initial embedding process, not a downstream analysis. It would give Kestrel a signal that any neuroscientist would immediately recognize as comparable to their data: regularly-sampled high-dimensional time series from two coupled sources, ready for all the standard tools without methodological asterisks.
Whether it's worth doing now or after we see the turn-level results is a judgment call. The turn-level analysis might be sufficient to demonstrate the findings. The chunk-level analysis would be the rigorous version for publication — the version where we can say "our preprocessing mirrors established neural signal analysis pipelines" and mean it.
