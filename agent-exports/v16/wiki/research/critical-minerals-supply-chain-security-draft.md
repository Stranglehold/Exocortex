## Overview

Critical minerals beyond rare earth elements that underpin the global energy transition, defense industrial base, and semiconductor manufacturing supply chains. Focus on lithium, cobalt, graphite, and other battery-critical materials where geopolitical concentration creates systemic risk.

## Supply Concentration Risk (2025-2026)

### Lithium
- **Demand projection:** IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 forecasts lithium demand could triple by 2030 under net-zero pathway [Source: IEA GCMO 2025]
- **Processing concentration:** Australia leads mining but China dominates refining (60%+ of global lithium processing capacity) [Source: IEA GCMO 2025]
- **Western alternatives:** Lithium Americas Thacker Pass (Nevada) progressing; SQM projects in Argentina; Greenbushes expansion

### Cobalt
- **Geographic concentration:** DRC produces ~70% of global cobalt
- **Refining bottleneck:** China processes ~75% through hydrometallurgical refining
- **Substitution trend:** LFP battery chemistry reducing cobalt dependency in EV applications

### Graphite (Battery-Grade Spherical)
- **Highest concentration risk:** China controls ~90% of spherical graphite processing capacity [Source: IEA GCMO 2025]
- **Western gap:** No significant Western spherical graphite processing capacity as of 2025

### Nickel
- **Tier divergence:** Class 1 nickel adequate; Class 2 faces glut from Indonesian HPAL projects
- **Geographic shift:** Indonesia surpassed New Guinea as top producer; 60%+ new capacity Indonesia-based

## Processing vs. Mining: The Real Bottleneck

**Key finding from IEA GCMO 2025:** Mining capacity is not the primary constraint — processing and refining are.

| Mineral | Mining Concentration | Processing Concentration | Primary Processor |
|---------|---------------------|-------------------------|-------------------|
| Lithium | Moderate (Australia, Chile) | High (China ~60%+) | China |
| Cobalt | Extreme (DRC ~70%) | Extreme (China ~75%) | China |
| Graphite (spherical) | Moderate | Extreme (China ~90%) | China |
| Rare Earths | Moderate (MP, Lynas) | Extreme (China ~85%+) | China |

Export controls on processing capacity are more strategically significant than raw material export controls.

## Western Alternative Supply Chains

### Lynas Rare Earths (Australia)
- Mountain Pass feedstock processing in Australia + Kalgoorlie separation facility
- U.S. Rare Earths Processing Facility under construction (Texas, DOE-funded)
- **Limitation:** Still dependent on Chinese refining for some heavy rare earths

### MP Materials (Mountain Pass, California)
- U.S. took controlling stake in 2025
- Full separation capability achieved (light rare earths)
- Hydrogen plant for magnet manufacturing (downstream integration)
- **Gap:** Heavy rare earth separation still limited

### Other Western Projects
- **Raven Resources (Canada):** NdPr project; pre-production in 2026
- **Green Metals Refining (Namibia):** N$1 billion Walvis Bay acid plant (Sep 2025)
- **Lithium Americas Thacker Pass:** Nevada lithium mine progressing toward production

**Assessment (Materials Dispatch, 2026):** Ex-China supply chain is "beginning to look more structured, but remains thin, concentrated, and exposed to a small number of highly strategic assets."

## Recycling Economics

- **Current state:** Battery recycling economics improving but not yet competitive with virgin material at scale
- **Closed-loop potential:** 90%+ material recovery technically feasible
- **Policy drivers:** EU Battery Regulation mandates recycled content; IRA domestic processing bonuses
- **Limitation:** Recycling addresses demand growth partially — absolute demand increase outpaces recyclable supply
