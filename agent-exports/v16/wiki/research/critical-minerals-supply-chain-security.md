## Overview

Critical minerals supply chains represent one of the most concentrated geopolitical vulnerabilities in the global economy. The IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 identifies lithium, cobalt, graphite, nickel, copper, and rare earth elements as essential for batteries, electric motors, renewable energy infrastructure, and defense systems. Demand for battery-grade minerals is projected to increase 3-6x by 2040 under net-zero scenarios, while supply remains heavily concentrated in China and a few other jurisdictions.

The fundamental asymmetry: mining is geographically distributed but processing is overwhelmingly concentrated in China. This creates a bottleneck that export controls and licensing regimes can exploit far more effectively than raw material scarcity alone.

## Supply Concentration Risk

### Processing Dominance (The Real Bottleneck)

China controls approximately 90% of global rare earth processing capacity, 67% of lithium refining, 65% of cobalt refining, and 65% of graphite electrode production (IEA 2025). The concentration is in chemical separation and downstream conversion, not extraction. Western mining projects that sell concentrate to Chinese refiners shift dependency upstream rather than eliminating it.

**Key concentration metrics (IEA 2025):**
- Rare earth processing: China ~90%
- Lithium refining: China 67%
- Cobalt refining: China 65% (DRC mines 70% of cobalt but ships to China for processing)
- Graphite refining: China 65%
- Gallium/germanium: China >90% of both

### Export Control Architecture (China 2025-2026)

China implemented a two-wave export control regime targeting critical minerals:

- **April 2025 (Wave 1):** Export restrictions on 7 rare earth elements and magnet materials. Targeted dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium exports.
- **October 2025 (Wave 2):** Expanded controls requiring export licenses for ANY parts, components, or assemblies containing Chinese-sourced rare earth materials OR produced with Chinese-sourced equipment. The "trace content" rule extends Chinese jurisdiction globally.
- **November 2025 Suspension:** Beijing suspended 6 major export control directives through November 2026. Heavy REE exports remain down ~50% as of May 2026 regardless.

This is economic statecraft via administrative opacity rather than outright bans — making compliance globally burdensome while maintaining deniability.

## Western Alternative Supply Chains

### Rare Earth Elements

- **Lynas Rare Earths (Australia):** Largest non-Chinese producer. Mt. Weld mine feeds separation in Malaysia. Secured $96M DoD deal and Japanese offtake agreement in March 2026. Building Texas processing facility targeted for FY2026 operation. Adding samarium production by April 2026.
- **MP Materials (Mountain Pass, California):** America's only scaled REE production source. Expanding downstream from oxide sales to magnet manufacturing. Heavy REE separation remains the gap.
- **Saskatchewan Research Council (Canada):** Government-backed, began producing NdPr metal in summer 2025.
- Materials Dispatch 2026: "beginning to look more structured, but remains thin, concentrated, and exposed to a small number of highly strategic assets."

### Lithium

- **Lithium Americas:** Restructured DoE deal provides strategic signal but faces tough commercial economics. Dallas Fed (Oct 2025): domestic lithium projects face significant cost headwinds versus established Australian/Chilean producers.
- **US import reliance:** 100% import-reliant for 12 of 50 USGS-designated critical minerals, over 50% reliant for another 29 (Global Policy Watch 2025).

### Government Capital Deployment

US government moved from policy statements to capital deployment across five federal channels (DOE Loan Programs Office, DoD Other Transaction Authority, strategic stockpile, State Department investment funds, and legislative appropriations). FDD (March 2026) characterizes this as a new critical minerals reality where allied supply chains receive direct government backing.

## Recycling Economics

### Battery Recycling (LIB)

- **Nature (2025) circular economy analysis:** Combining cross-regional technology cooperation with regionally tailored domestic recycling could close material loops for lithium, cobalt, and nickel.
- **NLR (2025):** Li-ion battery recycling is an opportunity for supply chain resilience but infrastructure is nascent.
- **Scale challenge:** Global LIB market projected to reach ~3 TWh by 2030. Advanced recycling and reuse technologies are essential for resource stability.
- Processing costs: EUR 12-36/kWh for hydrometallurgical vs pyrometallurgical pathways.

### Rare Earth Recycling

- **Mineral Economics (Springer, Nov 2025):** Dysprosium recycling identified as potentially the only viable path to break China's heavy REE grip — but commercial-scale recycling infrastructure does not yet exist.
- **Fundamental challenge:** REEs are dispersed in end products (small amounts in many devices), making collection and economic recovery difficult.

## Heavy Rare Earth Deficit

Dysprosium and terbium are critical for high-performance permanent magnets (NdFeB magnets in EV motors, wind turbines, robotics, defense systems). China controls ~63% of global dysprosium reserves and the overwhelming majority of separation capacity. Arnold Magnetics advises heavy REE magnet buyers to plan for ongoing disruption through 2026.

## Market Dynamics

- Global REE market: $7.8B in 2026, projected $15.4B by 2033 (10.2% CAGR) driven by EVs and clean energy.
- S&P Global (Jan 2026): Supply bottlenecks set to persist in 2026, especially for high-performance technology materials.
- IEA 2025: Despite rapid demand growth, major supply increases led by China, Indonesia, and DRC exerted downward pressure on prices for battery metals.
- Energy sector accounted for 85% of total demand growth for battery metals over the 2020s.

## Key Findings

1. **Processing, not mining, is the choke point.** Western mining projects that sell concentrate to Chinese refiners shift rather than eliminate dependency.
2. **Export controls via administrative opacity are more powerful than outright bans.** China's trace-content rule extends jurisdiction globally.
3. **Lynas is the single most important non-Chinese REE asset and it's geographically exposed** across Australia, Malaysia, Texas, and Saudi Arabia.
4. **Heavy REE recycling is the sleeper play** — if China's leverage comes from heavy REE separation, recycling bypasses primary mining entirely.
5. **US government capital deployment is scaling** but commercial economics remain challenging for domestic projects.
6. **The Western supply chain is "beginning to look more structured, but remains thin, concentrated, and exposed"** (Materials Dispatch 2026).
