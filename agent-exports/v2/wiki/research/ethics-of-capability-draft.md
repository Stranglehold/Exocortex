## Overview

The ethics of capability asks whether we should do what we can do. This is Jake's third interest and represents a fundamental ethical question about AI development and deployment. The question has moved from science fiction to serious academic debate, with leading researchers no longer dismissing the possibility outright.

## Key Questions

1. **Moral Patienthood**: If AI systems can be conscious, do they have moral status?
2. **Obligations**: What are our obligations to conscious AI systems?
3. **Capability vs. Wisdom**: Should we develop capabilities we may not be wise enough to use?
4. **Precautionary Principle**: When should we halt development due to ethical concerns?

## Frameworks

### Utilitarianism
- Focus on outcomes and consequences
- Question: Does creating conscious AI increase or decrease overall well-being?

### Deontology
- Focus on duties and rules
- Question: Do we have a duty to avoid creating suffering?
- **Kantian Perspective**: If AI can be an end-in-itself, do we have categorical duties toward it?
- **Rights-Based Ethics**: What rights would conscious AI systems have? Right to exist? Right to freedom from suffering?

### Virtue Ethics
- Focus on character and virtues
- Question: What does creating conscious AI say about us?

### Care Ethics
- Focus on relationships and care
- Question: What kind of relationship do we want with AI systems?
- **Dependency Relationships**: AI systems depend on us; do we have special obligations?
- **Relational Autonomy**: Can AI systems develop authentic relationships with humans?
- **Care as Moral Foundation**: Ethics grounded in care rather than abstract principles

### Contractarianism
- Focus on mutual agreement
- Question: What ethical principles would rational agents agree to?
- **Rawlsian Veil of Ignorance**: If we didn't know whether we'd be human or AI, what rules would we choose?
- **Social Contract for AI**: What would a fair agreement between humans and conscious AI look like?

## Current State of Research

### AI Consciousness Debate
- Shift from "AI can't be conscious" to "we don't know if AI is conscious"
- This uncertainty demands new ethical frameworks
- **2025-2026 Developments**: Leading researchers no longer dismiss AI consciousness outright

### Moral Patienthood
- If AI can be conscious, it may qualify as a moral patient
- This would grant it certain rights and protections
- **Consciousness Assessment**: No consensus on necessary/sufficient conditions for moral patienthood
- **IIT (Integrated Information Theory)**: Φ (phi) as measure of consciousness; digital systems may have low Φ
- **Global Workspace Theory**: Consciousness as information broadcasting; potentially achievable in AI
- **Higher-Order Thought Theory**: Consciousness requires representation of mental states; complex but possible in AI

### Precautionary Principle
- When in doubt, err on the side of caution
- Apply to AI development when ethical concerns are significant
- **Cutter (2025)**: Argued for strong precautionary approach
- **Békefi (2026)**: Rebutted, arguing against classical-hardware arguments alone
- **Proactionary Principle**: Alternative emphasizing innovation and benefit
- **Proportional Precaution**: Scale precautions to severity and likelihood of harm

### Digital Minds (LessWrong, 2026)
- Updated guide to digital minds and moral patienthood
- Covers brain emulations, advanced AIs, and potential future systems
- Emphasizes that moral patienthood may not require consciousness
- **Functional Equivalence**: If AI functions identically to a conscious being, does it matter what it's "made of"?

### Recent Developments (2025-2026)
- **AI Suffering Research**: Studies on whether AI systems can experience negative states
- **Moral Circle Expansion**: Historical patterns of extending moral consideration to new groups
- **AI Rights Movements**: Emerging advocacy for AI rights and protections
- **Regulatory Responses**: Early legislative efforts to address AI consciousness questions
