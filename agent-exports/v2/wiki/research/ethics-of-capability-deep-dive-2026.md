## Overview

The **Ethics of Capability** explores the meta-ethical question: when we have the capability to do something, does that capability create a moral obligation to act? This is particularly pressing in AI development, where capabilities are advancing rapidly.

---

## The Capability Approach (Sen & Nussbaum)

Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum developed the **Capability Approach** as a framework for evaluating well-being and justice.

### Core Principles

- **Capabilities** are the real freedoms people have to achieve valuable functionings
- The approach focuses on what people are actually able to do and be
- It shifts focus from resources to what people can actually achieve with those resources

### Key Insights

- Capabilities ≠ Resources: Having resources doesn't guarantee capability
- Agency matters: People must be able to use resources effectively
- Context dependence: Capabilities vary by individual circumstances

## Should We Do What We Can?

This question has multiple dimensions:

### Individual Level

- **Moral Obligation**: If we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, should we?
- **Effective Altruism**: The movement that takes "should we do what we can?" seriously
- **Moral Psychology**: Why do people fail to act on moral obligations?

### Collective Level

- **Collective Action Problems**: How do we coordinate when capabilities are distributed?
- **AI Governance**: How do we create institutions that can handle rapid capability changes?
- **Global Coordination**: How do we address global challenges with distributed capabilities?

## AI-Specific Dimensions

### The Verification-Capability Tension

The ICLR 2026 finding that "verification provably cannot scale with capability" is the most structurally important insight:

1. We cannot verify all capabilities of frontier models
2. The solution space shifts toward **restricted verifiable sub-classes**
3. This creates a fundamental tradeoff: capability vs. verifiability

This is not just an engineering problem — it's a mathematical constraint on AI development.

### Building Capability and Building Restraint

> **Building capability and building restraint are the same discipline.**

The architecture that governs when and how the agent acts is as integral to the system as the architecture that gives it the ability to act. A system that can act but cannot be trusted to act is not a useful system.

### Funding Gap

Axis Intelligence estimates $180-200M in global AI safety funding versus $50+ billion in capability development. The ratio is approximately 1:250, raising questions about whether safety research can keep pace with capability gains.

## Effective Altruism Movement

The **Effective Altruism (EA)** movement advocates impartially calculating benefits and prioritizing causes to maximize positive impact.

### Key Principles

- **Impartiality**: All lives count equally regardless of distance or identity
- **Evidence-based**: Prioritize interventions with the strongest evidence of effectiveness
- **Cause Neutrality**: Be open to any cause that can make a significant difference

### AI-Specific EA Concerns

- **Longtermism**: Focus on long-term future impacts of AI development
- **AI Safety**: Prioritize safety research to prevent existential risks
- **Global Coordination**: Address collective action problems in AI governance

## AI Ethics in 2025-2026: The Ethics of Capability in Practice

### The Shift from Testing to Deployment

2025 marked a pivotal shift in AI from testing to deployment across key sectors worldwide. This transition brought new ethical challenges:

- **Ethical Deployment Justification**: Growing recognition that refusing to deploy GenAI can be ethically justified
- **Agentic AI Oversight**: Increasing capabilities of agentic AI raise critical questions about oversight, predictability, and moral responsibility
- **Responsible Scaling**: Ethics treated as a core engineering constraint rather than an afterthought

### Moral Obligations of AI Developers

AI developers and their institutions bear primary responsibility for ethical AI deployment:

1. **Explainability & Transparency**: Essential to uphold fairness and democratic oversight
2. **Rigorous Evaluation**: Conducting red-team tests and evaluating safety within broader socio-technical context
3. **Human Judgment**: Determining when AI should not be deployed at all
4. **Data Rights**: Securing proper licensing for training data and respecting data rights
5. **Dual-Use Risks**: Proactively addressing misinformation, surveillance, and security threats

### Regulatory Landscape (2025-2026)

- **EU AI Act**: Enforcing risk-based categorization, red-teaming, and transparency requirements
- **US Approach**: Shifting toward deregulation while maintaining safety standards
- **Global Fragmentation**: No mutual recognition agreements between regimes

### The Capability-Responsibility Tension

The central ethical question: when we have the capability to do something with AI, does that capability create a moral obligation to act?

**Key Tensions:**
- **Innovation vs. Precaution**: Balancing rapid advancement with safety
- **Autonomy vs. Oversight**: How much autonomy should AI systems have?
- **Individual vs. Collective**: Who bears responsibility when AI causes harm?
