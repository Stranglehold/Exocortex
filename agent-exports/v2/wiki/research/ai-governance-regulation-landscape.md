## Overview

The global AI governance landscape is fragmenting into three competing regulatory regimes with fundamentally different philosophies. As of May 2026, the enforcement gap between regulatory text and operational capability remains the defining challenge.

## The Three Regimes

### 1. EU AI Act — Precautionary Risk-Based Framework

**Legal basis:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

**Key dates:**
- Entered into force: August 1, 2024
- Prohibited practices banned: February 2, 2025
- **High-risk AI obligations binding: August 2, 2026** (Articles 9-17 provider requirements; Article 26 deployer requirements)

**Enforcement mechanism:**
- Fines up to €35 million or 7% of global annual revenue (whichever higher)
- National competent authorities designated by member states
- Conformity assessment required for high-risk systems (Annex III sectors)
- CE marking for compliant systems


**Omnibus VII Amendment (May 7, 2026):** EU Council and Parliament reached provisional agreement on AI Act amendments. Key changes:
- High-risk AI compliance deadline extended from August 2, 2026 to December 2027 (18-month extension)
- Transparency obligations still enter force August 2026
- Reduced overlap of rules, new prohibitions added
- Draft high-risk AI guidelines published by European Commission post-Omnibus
- Source: Council press release May 7 2026; CSA Research Note update; Hunton Andrews Kurth analysis

**Readiness gap:** CSA Research Note (May 2026) documents significant enterprise readiness gap for high-risk compliance. Most organizations have not completed risk assessments, data governance audits, or conformity assessment preparation.

### 2. United States — Sectoral Enforcement + Voluntary Framework

**Legal basis:** No comprehensive federal AI law. De facto compliance through:

- **NIST AI RMF 1.0** (Feb 2024) — 72 subcategories across Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Gaining operational weight as negligence defense standard (Arnold & Porter, Jan 2026).
- **Executive Order 14110** — Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy AI. Implementation through agency guidance rather than binding rulemaking.
- **Sectoral enforcement:**
  - FDA: AI/ML-enabled medical devices (Preliminary AI/ML Action Plan)
  - FINRA: AI in financial advisory and trading
  - FTC: Unfair/deceptive AI practices enforcement actions
  - CFPB: AI in consumer financial products
- **State laws:** Fragmenting patchwork (CA, CO, IA, DE leading on AI disclosure and liability)

**Enforcement reality:** Messier than EU. 
**White House National Policy Framework (March 20, 2026):** Non-binding legislative recommendations to Congress covering 8 policy areas. Builds on EO 14365 (Dec 11, 2025) "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" which challenges state AI laws that may obstruct national strategy. Key shift: federal preemption push against state-level patchwork (CSET analysis March 2026; Vorys April 2026). Executive orders pull one direction, state legislatures push another, agencies enforce existing laws against AI companies. Compliance obligations depend on jurisdiction, sector, and system function (VerifyWise, 2026).

### 3. China — State-Aligned Development with Mandatory Filing

**Legal basis:**
- **Interim Measures for Generative AI Services** (July 2023)
**2026 Unified AI Law (Draft, May 2026):** State Council confirmed in 2026 legislative work plan that Beijing is accelerating work on first comprehensive AI law to consolidate fragmented sector-specific rules. AI Governance Code 2026 issued by MOST with national standards for algorithmic transparency, data management, and responsible innovation. Cybersecurity Law 2025 amendment (effective Jan 1, 2026) explicitly references AI research and governance. China AI compliance framework for digital platforms effective July 15, 2026. — First specific administrative regulation on GenAI
- **Algorithm Recommendation Filing** — Mandatory for both generative and non-generative AI
- **Deep Synthesis Provisions** — Synthetic media labeling requirements
- **PIPL** (Personal Information Protection Law) — Data export controls

**Enforcement data:**
- January–February 2026: 48 new GenAI services + 46 AI applications completed filing/registration (CAC announcement, March 17, 2026)
- **2026 AI Governance Framework** under final drafting — moving from sector-specific guidelines toward unified system regulating algorithmic design, data use, and ethical deployment
- Alignment with socialist core values required for all GenAI training data

**Strategic dimension:** China mandates pre-approval of algorithms and enforces alignment with state ideologies, highlighting the geopolitical dimension of AI governance (White & Case LLP tracker).

## AI Liability — Emerging Enforcement Frontier

**Litigation data (GWU DAIL — Database of AI Litigation):**
- 212 total AI lawsuits cataloged
- 64 involve GenAI specifically
- 11 involve design defects, failure to warn, negligence, or product liability

**Notable cases:**
- **Nippon Life v. OpenAI** (Mar 2026) — Stanford Law identifies as landmark product liability case. "Architectural negligence attaches to what the system was permitted to say."
- **Character Technologies wrongful death** — AI chatbot mental health harm litigation (Florida)
- **St. Clair v. X.AI Holdings** (S.D.N.Y. 2026) — Deepfake image harm
- **Nine pending cases** against OpenAI/GPT-4o alleging wrongful death and product liability

**Liability framework split:** Product liability (strict liability for defects) vs. service liability (negligence standard). NIST AI RMF compliance emerging as negligence defense in professional services context.

## Convergence vs. Fragmentation

**Convergence signals:**
- OECD AI Principles adopted by 42+ countries (non-binding)
- ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 developing AI standardization
- IEEE P7000 series on ethical AI

**Fragmentation signals:**
- UK pro-innovation approach contrasts with EU precautionary model
- China's state-alignment requirements incompatible with Western frameworks
- US state-level patchwork creating compliance fragmentation
- No mutual recognition agreements between regimes

**Assessment:** Fragmentation is the current trajectory. Regulatory regimes are becoming instruments of tech statecraft rather than converging on common standards.
