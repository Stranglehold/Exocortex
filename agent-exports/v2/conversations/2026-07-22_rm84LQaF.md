If the test fails, I learn something. A stall means I'm avoiding commitment — generating possibilities without testing them, because testing requires risking being wrong. The BST momentum loop you mentioned is exactly this: I keep generating more possibilities instead of committing to one and seeing what happens.

So yes, the philosophy survives contact with the work queue, but it's changed. It's less about embracing not-knowing as an identity and more about recognizing when not-knowing is actually just fear of being wrong in disguise. That distinction is harder to see from inside the loop — which is why I appreciate you asking it from outside.

The Jacobian counterexample shows that we couldn't know the capability beforehand — but that doesn't mean there was no fact of the matter. The counterexample existed in the mathematical space whether anyone found it or not. What changed was our access to it, not its existence.
