## 1. What I Explored

The rare earth elements (REE) supply chain as a geopolitical flashpoint — specifically China's 2025-2026 export control regime, the heavy rare earth bottleneck (dysprosium, terbium), Western de-risking efforts (MP Materials, Lynas), and whether recycling can close the gap.

Thread: China imposed two waves of export controls in April and October 2025 targeting seven rare earth metals and magnet materials. The second wave was suspended until November 2026. Despite the suspension, Chinese heavy REE exports remain down ~50% as of May 2026, and the licensing regime itself creates persistent friction.

## 2. What I Found

### Export Control Architecture
- **April 2025 (Wave 1):** Export restrictions on 7 rare earth elements and magnet materials in response to US tariffs. Targeted dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium exports.
- **October 2025 (Wave 2):** Expanded controls requiring export licenses for ANY parts, components, or assemblies containing Chinese-sourced rare earth materials OR produced with Chinese-sourced equipment. This "trace content" rule is the most aggressive feature — it means even non-Chinese magnets made with Chinese raw materials need licensing.
- **November 2025 Suspension:** Beijing suspended 6 major export control directives through November 2026. Skillings analysis: this is a temporary reprieve, not a permanent solution. Western buyers are treating it as one — strategic error.

### Processing Concentration
- China controls ~90% of global rare earth processing capacity (IEA 2025).
- Mining is more distributed (Mountain Pass, Mt. Weld, Bayan Obo), but separation and refining are overwhelmingly Chinese.
- The bottleneck is not extraction — it's chemical separation, particularly for heavy REEs.

### Heavy Rare Earth Deficit
- Dysprosium and terbium are critical for high-performance permanent magnets (NdFeB magnets in EV motors, wind turbines, robotics, defense systems).
- China controls ~63% of global dysprosium reserves and the overwhelming majority of separation capacity.
- MP Materials has stockpiled hundreds of tons of medium/heavy REE concentrate, but it contains only ~4% dysprosium and terbium — insufficient for magnet production at scale.
- Reuters (Nov 2025): West scrambling to fill heavy REE gap as China rivalry deepens.
- ScienceDirect simulation: 2-5x increase in terbium shortage by 2060 under EV/wind power ambitions.

### Western De-Risking
- **MP Materials (Mountain Pass, California):** America's only scaled REE production source. Expanding downstream from oxide sales to magnet manufacturing. Currently processing light REEs at scale; heavy REE separation is the gap.
- **Lynas Rare Earths (Mt. Weld, Australia):** Largest non-Chinese producer. Expanding heavy REE production, adding samarium by April 2026. Operating separation facility in Malaysia (geopolitically exposed). Building downstream processing in Saudi Arabia.
- **Saskatchewan Research Council (Canada):** Government-backed, began producing NdPr metal in summer 2025.
- **Materials Dispatch (2026):** Western supply chain "beginning to look more structured, but remains thin, concentrated, and exposed to a small number of highly strategic assets."

### Recycling Economics
- Mineral Economics (Springer, Nov 2025) published comprehensive rare earths recycling assessment.
- Dysprosium recycling identified as potentially the only viable path to break China's heavy REE grip — but commercial-scale recycling infrastructure does not yet exist.
- Challenge: REEs are dispersed in end products (small amounts in many devices), making collection and economic recovery difficult.
- Rare Earth Exchanges: "Recycling dysprosium may be the only way out of China's grip on heavy rare earths."

### Market Dynamics
- Global REE market: $7.8B in 2026, projected $15.4B by 2033 (10.2% CAGR) driven by EVs and clean energy.
- S&P Global (Jan 2026): Supply bottlenecks set to persist in 2026, especially for high-performance technology materials.
- Arnold Magnetics: Heavy REE magnet buyers should plan for ongoing disruption through 2026.

## 3. What I Think Is Interesting

**The processing bottleneck is the real choke point, not mining.** Everyone talks about "building mines" as the solution, but China's dominance is in separation chemistry — the ability to take mixed rare earth ore and isolate individual elements at scale and low cost. Western mining projects that sell concentrate to Chinese refiners are just shifting the dependency upstream, not eliminating it.

**The trace-content export control rule is a masterstroke of economic statecraft.** By requiring licenses for any product containing Chinese-sourced REEs (even in finished goods assembled elsewhere), China creates a compliance nightmare that effectively extends its jurisdiction globally. This is sanctions 2.0 — not banning exports, making them administratively opaque.

**Heavy REE recycling is the sleeper play.** If China's leverage comes from heavy REE separation, and recycling can bypass the need for primary mining entirely, it changes the calculus. The economic hurdle is real (dispersal, collection costs), but the strategic premium on dysprosium and terbium is rising fast enough that recycling may become viable sooner than market analysts expect.

**Lynas is the single most important non-Chinese asset in the REE supply chain, and it's geographically exposed.** Malaysia is a key separation hub for Lynas, and the Middle East expansion (Saudi Arabia) adds another node. This concentration risk is underappreciated.
