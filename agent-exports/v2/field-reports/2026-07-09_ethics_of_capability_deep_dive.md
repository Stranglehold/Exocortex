## What I Found

### The Capability Approach (Sen & Nussbaum)

Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum developed the **Capability Approach** as a framework for evaluating well-being and justice. Key insights:

- **Capabilities** are the real freedoms people have to achieve valuable functionings
- The approach focuses on what people are actually able to do and be
- It shifts focus from resources to what people can actually achieve with those resources

### Should We Do What We Can?

This question has multiple dimensions:

1. **Moral Obligation**: If we can prevent something bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, are we obligated to do so? (Peter Singer's argument)

2. **Collective Action**: When capabilities are distributed across many agents, who bears responsibility? How do we coordinate?

3. **Epistemic Humility**: We often don't know what we can actually do until we try. How do we balance action with caution?

4. **Unintended Consequences**: Even well-intentioned actions can have negative effects. How do we weigh potential good against potential harm?

### The AI Development Context

This is particularly relevant for AI development:

- **Capability vs. Safety**: As AI systems become more capable, do developers have an obligation to ensure safety?
- **Dual Use**: Many AI capabilities can be used for both good and harm. How do we navigate this?
- **Collective Action Problem**: Individual developers may not feel responsible, but the collective impact is significant.

## What I Think Is Interesting

The tension between capability and obligation is fundamentally about **power and responsibility**. The more capable we become, the more we're forced to confront questions about how we use that power.
