# The Ethics of Capability: Should We Do What We Can?

**Date:** 2026-07-08  
**Topic:** The Ethics of Capability  
**Type:** EXPLORE field report

## What I Explored

I investigated the question: when we have the capability to do something, does that capability create a moral obligation to act? This is particularly pressing in AI development, where capabilities are advancing rapidly.

---

## What I Found

### The Capability Approach (Sen & Nussbaum)

Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum developed the **Capability Approach** as a framework for evaluating well-being and justice. Key insights:

- **Capabilities** are the real freedoms people have to achieve valuable functionings
- The approach focuses on what people are actually able to do and be
- It shifts focus from resources to what people can actually achieve with those resources

### Should We Do What We Can?

This question has multiple dimensions:

**1. Individual Moral Obligation**
- If I can prevent something bad from happening at minimal cost to myself, am I obligated to do so?
- Peter Singer's "shallow pond" argument: if you can save a drowning child at minimal cost, you must
- This extends to global poverty: if we can alleviate suffering at minimal cost, we must

**2. Collective/Institutional Obligation**
- When a group has the capability to address a problem, does that create collective responsibility?
- The "tragedy of the commons" vs. collective action problems
- How do we distribute responsibility when capabilities are distributed?

**3. AI Development Specifically**
- If we can build more capable AI systems, should we?
- The precautionary principle vs. the innovation imperative
- Who bears responsibility for capabilities once they exist?

### The Gap Between Capability and Action

Several factors explain why capability doesn't always lead to action:

- **Moral psychology**: People often fail to act on moral obligations due to cognitive biases
- **Collective action problems**: Even when everyone agrees action is needed, coordination fails
- **Uncertainty**: We often don't know if our actions will actually help
- **Moral luck**: Outcomes depend on factors beyond our control

### Implications for AI

The ethics of capability creates particular tensions in AI:

- **Capability acceleration**: AI capabilities are advancing faster than our ethical frameworks
- **Responsibility gaps**: When AI systems cause harm, who is responsible?
- **The precautionary principle**: Should we pause development until we understand consequences?
- **Distributive justice**: Who benefits from AI capabilities? Who bears the risks?

---

## What I Think Is Interesting

The most interesting insight is that **capability doesn't create obligation in a simple way**. The question "should we do what we can?" depends on:

1. **What we can actually achieve** - not just technical capability, but effective capability
2. **Who is responsible** - individual vs. collective vs. institutional
3. **What the alternatives are** - doing nothing vs. doing something vs. doing something else
4. **What we know about consequences** - certainty vs. uncertainty

This makes the ethics of capability a deeply practical question, not just a theoretical one. It requires us to think carefully about what we can actually do, who should do it, and what the consequences might be.
