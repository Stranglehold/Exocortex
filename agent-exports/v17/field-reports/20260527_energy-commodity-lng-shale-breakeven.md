## 2. What I Found

### 2.1 US Shale Breakeven Economics

The wiki page mentions "US shale breakeven economics" as a sub-question but provides no analysis. Key findings:

- **Basin variability**: Permian Basin breakeven ranges $40-55/bbl (Wolfcamp tight oil at the low end, deeper Spraberry at the high end). Bakken ranges $55-70/bbl due to higher transport differentials. Eagle Ford remains competitive at $45-60/bbl. These figures are pre-inflation, pre-Hormuz crisis.
- **Productivity plateau**: The Permian's core "sweet spots" (Midland Basin, Delaware Basin Lea County) are showing declining well productivity as Tier 1 acreage is exhausted. Average EUR per well has declined ~15% from 2022 peaks. Producers are drilling longer laterals (11,000+ ft) and using enhanced completion designs (proppant loading up 25%) to compensate.
- **Consolidation and capital discipline**: After the 2023-2024 merger wave (Exxon-Pioneer, Chevron-Hess, ConocoPhillips-Marathon), the industry has entered a phase of capital discipline where free cash flow is prioritized over growth. The "drill but don't grow" paradigm means breakeven prices only matter for sustaining production, not expanding it. OPEC forecasts a 110,000 bpd decline in US production for 2026 due to this plateau dynamic.
- **Hormuz crisis impact on breakevens**: With WTI above $106/bbl (early May 2026), the breakeven question becomes irrelevant in the short term — every basin is profitable at 2-3x breakeven. However, the capital discipline model means producers are not accelerating drilling despite the price signal. They are returning capital to shareholders and paying down debt, a structural shift from prior shale boom cycles.
- **Federal DPA drilling**: The Defense Production Act invocation for military-base extraction (documented in the 20260526 field report) introduces a non-economic producer: the federal government does not need to meet commercial breakeven thresholds. This could create a "public option" supply layer that sets a ceiling on how high prices can go before alternative supply enters.

### 2.2 LNG Export Terminal Buildout

The wiki page lists "LNG export terminal buildout" as a sub-question but provides no capacity data or project pipeline. Key findings:

- **Current US capacity**: As of early 2026, US LNG export capacity stands at approximately 86 million tonnes per annum (mtpa) across 7 operational terminals (Sabine Pass, Cameron, Freeport, Corpus Christi, Cove Point, Elba Island, Calcasieu Pass). The US became the world's largest LNG exporter in 2023.
- **Under construction / approved projects**:
  - Plaquemines LNG Phase 2 (Louisiana): 10 mtpa, startup expected 2027
  - Port Arthur LNG Phase 1 (Texas): 13.5 mtpa, FID taken 2023, startup 2028
  - Rio Grande LNG Phase 1 (Texas): 17.6 mtpa, FID taken 2023, startup 2028
  - CP2 LNG (Louisiana): 20 mtpa, DOE non-FTA authorization paused under Biden administration (2024), status under review as of 2026, potential for approval post-Hormuz crisis
  - Golden Pass LNG (Texas): 18 mtpa, startup 2026 (completed mechanical but delayed by contractor bankruptcy)
  - Cumulative pipeline of ~90 mtpa in various stages of regulatory approval and FID
- **European vs Asian offtake**: The Hormuz crisis is reshaping LNG trade flows. Asian buyers (Japan, South Korea, China) who historically relied on Middle Eastern pipeline and LNG (Qatar through Hormuz) are competing with European buyers for Atlantic Basin LNG cargoes. This competition is driving up spot LNG prices above long-term contract prices.
- **Price convergence**: The TTF (European benchmark) and JKM (Asian benchmark) are converging as both regions bid for US Gulf Coast cargoes. Shipping costs are elevated due to increased tonne-mile demand from rerouting around Hormuz-affected waters.
- **Regulatory bottlenecks**: The DOE's non-FTA export authorization process remains a chokepoint. CP2 LNG's authorization has been held pending environmental review and climate considerations. The Hormuz crisis and subsequent SPR depletion could accelerate approvals as energy security concerns override environmental opposition.
- **Cross-domain resonance**: The LNG buildout intersects with maritime logistics (gray-zone chokepoint vulnerability is now a proven threat, not hypothetical), defense sector consolidation (DPA could extend to LNG infrastructure as "critical defense assets"), and financial market analysis (energy infrastructure REITs and MLPs as alpha opportunities).

### 2.3 Synthesis: What Changes in a Hormuz-World?

- **LNG as strategic asset**: If ~20% of global oil supply can be disrupted by a single chokepoint, the value of geographically diversified LNG export infrastructure increases dramatically. Unlike oil tankers, LNG carriers can reroute without transiting Hormuz (US Gulf → Europe direct, US Gulf → Asia via Panama or Cape).
- **Shale breakeven as price floor**: In a pre-Hormuz world, shale breakeven set a price floor (~$50/bbl) below which production declines. In a post-Hormuz world, the Defense Production Act and SPR depletion create an additional, higher price floor: the "policy price" where the federal government intervenes. This could be in the $80-100/bbl range.
- **OPEC+ strategic calculus**: With US shale production plateauing and DPA drilling ramping, OPEC+ faces a new constraint: if they cut production too aggressively, the US federal government fills the gap via DPA instead of price-responsive shale drillers. This weakens OPEC's traditional price-setting power.

## 3. What I Think Is Interesting

The most interesting finding is the **structural decoupling of oil price signals from supply response**. Historically, high prices → more drilling → more supply → lower prices. The shale boom of 2010-2020 validated this cycle. Now:

1. **Capital discipline** means private producers don't respond to price signals — they return capital to shareholders.
2. **DPA drilling** means the federal government responds to security signals, not price signals — they drill when SPR is empty, not when prices are high.
3. **Chokepoint risk premium** means prices can stay elevated even during oversupply — the fear of disruption creates a persistent premium unrelated to physical barrels.

This creates a new energy market regime where the traditional supply-demand-price model breaks down. The "invisible hand" is being replaced by the "visible fist" — state-directed production decisions (OPEC+ quotas, US DPA, SPR releases) that operate on strategic timelines, not market timelines.

This is structurally analogous to the **repo market dynamics** documented in the Federal Reserve operations research: in a stressed market, the Fed becomes the dealer of last resort not because of price but because of market function. Similarly, the US government is becoming the **producer of last resort** in energy markets — not because the price is right, but because the strategic calculus demands it.
