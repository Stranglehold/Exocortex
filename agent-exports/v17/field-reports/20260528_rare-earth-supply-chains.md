## 1. What I Explored

Rare earth supply chains as a geopolitical vulnerability vector in 2026. China's processing monopoly—particularly for heavy rare earths (Dy, Tb) essential to EV motors, wind turbines, F-35 avionics, and missile guidance—is the central chokepoint. This cycle followed: the 2025 Concordia University full-value-chain review, S&P Global market reporting on China's dual-use export restrictions, the CFR's "Leapfrogging China's Critical Minerals Dominance" report, and the AZOMining 2026 commercial landscape overview.

## 2. What I Found

### The Refining Monopoly — Not Mining — Is the Real Choke

- **China controls 61% of global mined supply but 91% of refining and processing capacity** (2024 IEA data). For heavy rare earths (Dy, Tb), this approaches 99%.
- Mining is diversifying (US Mountain Pass, Australia Lynas, Myanmar, Brazil, Africa) but the midstream—chemical separation, purification, oxide finishing—remains almost entirely Chinese.
- Rare earth separation requires hundreds of extraction stages per element, often handling radioactive thorium/uranium byproducts. No Western country has restored large-scale separation capacity.
- **Concordia University review** (Gejjiganahalli Ningappa et al., 2025): "When one country dominates refining, the entire global technology stack becomes exposed."

### 2025-2026 Export Controls Escalation

- April 2025: China launched strict licensing regime limiting rare earth exports for defense/high-tech sectors.
- July 2025: Partial easing after US pushback.
- **January 2026: New dual-use export controls targeting Japan**—one of the few non-Chinese producers of advanced rare earth permanent magnets (~15% global share).
- Japan embassy official to S&P Global: "If Japan cannot import necessary rare earths for magnet production, it will eventually affect all downstream companies in the global supply chain."
- The ripple effect: restricting exports to Japan indirectly cuts magnet supply for EV makers and defense contractors worldwide.

### Western Alternatives: Processing Build-Out and Leapfrog Strategies

- **MP Materials** (US): Operating Mountain Pass mine, expanding beyond extraction into processing and magnet manufacturing. Positioning as integrated Western supplier.
- **Lynas Rare Earths** (Australia/Malaysia): Among the few non-Chinese processors. Expanding capacity but still limited relative to Chinese scale.
- **Globe Metals & Mining**: "The real pinch point is processing, refining, and qualification. For rare earths, that shows up most clearly in magnet materials and heavies."
- **CFR recommendations**: The US cannot out-mine and out-process China. Instead: scale disruptive innovation, recovery, and recycling. Key pillars:
  - Rare-earth-free and reduced-content magnets (materials engineering)
  - Waste-based recovery: extract REEs from mine tailings, industrial waste, end-of-life products
  - Close the scale-up financing gap for frontier mineral technologies
  - Embed innovation-led mineral security into allied frameworks

### Recycling Potential

- **Less than 5% of rare earths are recycled** despite 90%+ recovery potential (Concordia review).
- This represents a massive untapped domestic supply source that bypasses the Chinese processing chokepoint entirely.
- End-of-life EV motors, hard drives, and wind turbine magnets contain recoverable Nd, Dy, Tb at concentrations far exceeding virgin ore.

### Market Dynamics

- Prices for heavy rare earths (Dy, Tb) expected to remain elevated through 2026 due to export restrictions and limited alternative processing.
- Industry participants call for non-Chinese rare earth pricing benchmarks to reduce price discovery vulnerability.
- Supply bottlenecks for heavies are structural, not cyclical—requiring years to resolve even with aggressive Western investment.

## 3. What I Think Is Interesting

### The Midstream-Is-Everything Pattern

This is the most striking insight, and it generalizes far beyond rare earths. The pattern: the chokepoint is never where most people look (mining/reserves). It's always in the **processing step that transforms raw material into usable intermediate form**. In rare earths, that's chemical separation. In data, that's entity normalization and record linkage (the equivalent of refining heterogeneous raw records into a clean identity graph). In intelligence, it's the analytic processing that turns SIGINT intercepts into actionable reports.

This pattern appears repeatedly across domains Jake cares about: data aggregation/entity resolution, OSINT investigation methodology, hardware supply chains, and critical infrastructure. The lesson: when analyzing any system for vulnerability, look past the visible inputs and identify the invisible transformation step that turns crude inputs into finished components.

### The Recycling Arbitrage

<5% recycling with 90%+ technical recovery potential is a staggering gap—equivalent to a market consistently mispricing an asset by 18x. The economics are suppressed by: (1) insufficient collection infrastructure, (2) lack of standardized disassembly for rare-earth-containing products, (3) Chinese processing dominance that makes virgin material artificially cheap. If any of these barriers shift (e.g., export controls making virgin material expensive enough), the recycling economics flip rapidly. This is a coiled-spring dynamic.

### The ArXiv Null Result

A search for "rare earth supply chain" on ArXiv returned zero relevant economics/policy papers—only materials science (ruby lattice antiferromagnets, cryogenic noise metrology, supergravity). This suggests rare earth supply chain analysis lives primarily in policy institutes (CFR), industry research (S&P Global), and corporate reports—not academic CS/economics literature. For intelligence collection, this means: don't search ArXiv for market structure; search policy institute publications, trade journals (Platts, Argus), and corporate investor presentations.
