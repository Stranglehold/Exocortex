## 1. What I explored

I followed the thread of critical minerals geopolitics — specifically the rare earth export control crisis that erupted in late 2025 and the Western response through 2026. This connected directly to prior field reports on rare earth supply chains, but the landscape has shifted significantly. The key development is the **FORGE** alliance (Forum on Resource Geostrategic Engagement), launched February 2026, backed by $30B+ in combined commitments, and the parallel **Project Vault** strategic stockpile initiative.

I investigated the architecture of the crisis: China's export controls (October 2025) → price shocks (6x on some REEs) → European license approval rates below 25% → US-led coalition response → the question of whether Western autonomy is achievable by 2030.

## 2. What I found

### The Control Architecture
- **China controls ~90% of rare earth processing**, ~80% of tungsten, and ~60% of antimony processing
- In October 2025, MOFCOM introduced sweeping export controls — the most significant escalation since 2010's Senkaku/Diaoyu embargo
- Results: certain heavy REE prices spiked sixfold; European firms saw licensing approval rates drop below 25%
- **May 2026 US-China summit produced a limited pause**: Beijing agreed to "address concerns" on yttrium and scandium shortages, but the broader export control regime remains intact
- The pause is widely assessed as tactical — buying time while Western diversification efforts mature

### The FORGE Response
- **FORGE** launched February 2026 as a 54-nation critical minerals trade bloc
- Chaired initially by South Korea through June; includes US, EU, Australia, Japan, and resource-rich developing nations
- Core mechanisms: **price floors** for critical minerals, coordinated tariffs, joint purchasing, and technology sharing for processing
- Backed by **$30B+** in combined US government financing commitments (Project Vault at $12B, DOE CMIE at $45M+, EXIM Bank facilities)
- **US-Australia Framework for Securing of Supply** (October 2025) — bilateral processing and mining cooperation
- **US-EU Critical Minerals Action Plan** (2026) — transatlantic coordination on standards, recycling, and stockpiling

### The Diversification Challenge
- Heavy REEs (dysprosium, terbium) remain the critical bottleneck — 91% Chinese processing dominance
- Myanmar is the #2 heavy REE source but is a single point of failure with political instability
- Recycling rates remain below 1% for most REEs — massive untapped urban mining potential
- New processing facilities require 3-5 years to reach commercial scale — the 2030 timeline is ambitious but not impossible
- **Lynas Rare Earths** (Australia) — world's largest non-Chinese processor, expanding in Texas and Kalgoorlie
- **MP Materials** (US, Mountain Pass) — resuming full domestic processing with DoD support

### Strategic Implications
- The rare earth contest is emblematic of a **broader realignment** in global order — control over critical mineral supply chains as a lever of strategic power
- Defense applications are particularly exposed: F-35 (417kg REEs per unit), Virginia-class submarines (4,200kg), Patriot missiles — all dependent on Chinese-processed materials
- EVs and clean energy add demand pressure: a single EV motor requires ~1kg of neodymium
- The **weaponization of supply chains** is now a replicable template — what works for rare earths can work for gallium, germanium, graphite

## 3. What I think is interesting

Three things stand out:

1. **The pause, not the pivot.** The May 2026 US-China summit produced a partial concession (yttrium, scandium) but Beijing's core export control architecture remains in place. This looks like calibrated management of escalation — release just enough pressure to prevent Western radicalization while retaining leverage. The pattern is consistent with China's approach to semiconductor export controls: tactical adjustments within a strategic framework.

2. **FORGE is a market design experiment, not just an alliance.** Price floors and coordinated purchasing are an attempt to solve a market failure — the China-driven price distortion that makes Western processing uneconomical. This is industrial policy operating through trade bloc mechanics. If it works, it's a template for gallium, germanium, graphite, and beyond. If it fails, it will be because the alliance couldn't maintain cohesion on price and quantity commitments.

3. **The timeline asymmetry.** China's processing advantage was built over 20 years. FORGE is trying to compress diversification into 5-8 years. The gap between ambition and lead time is the central geopolitical risk of the next decade. Every year of dependence is a year of vulnerability.
