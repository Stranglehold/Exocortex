## 2. What I Found

### China's Midstream Dominance

China controls approximately 60% of global REE mining but **~90% of global processing and refining**. This is the critical distinction: mining is diversifying across the U.S. (Mountain Pass, CA), Australia, Myanmar, Brazil, and Africa, but the chemical separation and oxide finishing stages remain overwhelmingly Chinese.

A 2026 Concordia University review published in *Energy Storage Materials* (Ningappa et al.) frames this bluntly: "China controls more than 90% of global refining capacity, providing Beijing with unparalleled leverage over the clean-energy and defense economies of virtually every industrialized nation."

The true chokepoint is chemical separation, requiring hundreds to thousands of extraction stages per element. Rare earth ores contain chemically similar elements mixed with radioactive contaminants (thorium, uranium), making solvent extraction extraordinarily complex. No Western country has yet restored large-scale separation capacity.

**Heavy rare earths (HREEs) — dysprosium and terbium — are the most critical vulnerability.** They are indispensable for NdFeB magnets that maintain performance at high temperatures, essential for:
- F-35 fighter jets (900+ lbs of REEs per aircraft)
- Virginia-class submarines (9,200 lbs)
- Arleigh Burke destroyers (5,200 lbs)
- Tomahawk missiles, Predator drones, JDAM smart bombs
- EV motors and direct-drive wind turbines

China's December 2025 export controls on heavy REEs triggered emergency stockpiling and price surges across Western defense and clean energy supply chains in 2026.

### China's Aggressive Consolidation

In May 2025, Shenghe Resources (via Ganzhou Chenguang Rare Earths) acquired full ownership of Australia-based Peak Rare Earths' Tanzanian project for $158M AUD — a **200% premium** over Peak's May 9 closing price. CSIS cites this as evidence that Chinese companies are willing to significantly overpay to maintain global processing dominance.

### Western Diversification Efforts

**MP Materials (U.S.):**
- Operates Mountain Pass, CA — the only active U.S. rare earth mine, producing ~15% of global supply
- Until early 2024, shipped most domestically mined REEs to China for processing
- Now building Independence facility in Texas for domestic magnet manufacturing
- July 2025: landmark public-private partnership with Department of Defense
- $500M multi-year deal with Apple for domestically-made NdFeB magnets; dedicated recycling line starting ~2027

**Lynas Rare Earths (Australia):**
- The other major non-Chinese commercial producer
- Processing facility in Malaysia; expanding to Kalgoorlie, Western Australia
- Also received U.S. DoD funding for a heavy rare earth separation facility in Texas

**Minerals Security Partnership (MSP):**
- U.S.-led initiative backed Brazil's Serra Verde project — the only mine outside Asia producing both light and heavy rare earths
- However, CSIS notes the ore is "already locked into offtake agreements with China for processing" — because no Western processing alternative exists

### Recycling: Technical Promise, Economic Challenge

**Current state:** Less than 5% of global rare earths are recycled (under 1% in the U.S.), despite demonstrated technical potential for >90% recovery of Nd, Pr, Dy, Tb from magnet scrap.

**Three technology pathways:**

1. **Hydrogen decrepitation (magnet-to-magnet):** Injecting H₂ gas to crack sintered NdFeB magnets into powder without chemical breakdown. Preserves alloy integrity. Leaders: HyProMag (UK→US, U. Birmingham spinout), Noveon (USA).

2. **Hydrometallurgy/pyrometallurgy:** Chemical or thermal breakdown of magnets into oxides. Leaders: Phoenix Tailings (acid-free leach + molten salt electrolysis, zero-waste), Cyclic Materials (Canada, >90% recovery, backed by JLR/BMW).

3. **Advanced separation:** Ion-exchange, membrane extraction, bio-adsorbents. Leaders: ReElement Technologies (ion chromatography), REEcycle (electrochemical separation, 98% recovery of Nd/Pr/Dy/Tb — DoD funded $5.1M).

**Major initiatives:**
- Attero (India): scaling from 300 to 30,000 tonnes/year by 2027 (₹1B government backing, 98% recovery)
- EU Critical Raw Materials Act: mandates 25% of key magnet materials from recycling by 2030
- CREEM consortium (UK, £11M): Ionic Technologies + EMR + Ford/Bentley/Wrightbus
- RarEarth (Italy): €2.6M raised for first Italian NdFeB magnet factory from recycled e-motor waste

**Key challenge:** Chinese dominance in refining means recycled material still needs downstream processing. Western analysts: "no near-term substitute" exists for China's processing capacity. REE market projected to grow from $7.8B (2026) to $15.4B (2033) at 10.2% CAGR.

## 3. What I Think Is Interesting

### The Midstream Capture Pattern

This is not a resource scarcity problem — it's a processing capability problem. Rare earths are abundant in the earth's crust. The bottleneck is a single country's control over the chemical separation technology required to make them usable. This is structurally identical to the semiconductor supply chain, where TSMC's fabrication dominance (not silicon availability) is the chokepoint.

### Why Separation Is So Hard

The Concordia review highlights something the policy discourse often glosses over: rare earth separation requires hundreds to thousands of extraction stages per element because the lanthanides are chemically nearly identical. Add radioactive thorium/uranium contamination, and you have a process that requires decades of tacit knowledge accumulation, specialized workforce, and regulatory tolerance that China built over 30 years while the West offshored its processing.

### Recycling Economics vs. Geopolitics

The technology works. Hydrogen decrepitation recovers >90% of magnet alloy with massive energy savings. REEcycle achieves 98% recovery. But at current Chinese virgin material prices, recycling is uneconomical. This is classic dumping economics: China's processing scale and environmental externalities suppress global prices below what recycling can compete with. The solution is not just technology — it's industrial policy that internalizes the geopolitical risk premium into procurement decisions.

### The F-35 Dependency

An F-35 contains 900+ pounds of rare earth elements whose processing is almost entirely controlled by a potential adversary. This is a staggering concentration of strategic risk in a single supply chain node.

## 5. Cross-Domain Connections

### Semiconductor Supply Chain
Same structural pattern: manufacturing/processing is the bottleneck, not raw materials. TSMC's fab dominance mirrors China's REE refining dominance. The U.S. CHIPS Act and REE processing investments (DoD MP Materials partnership, MSP) are parallel industrial policy responses to the same midstream-capture problem.

### Agent Architecture
"Midstream bottleneck" is a general systems pattern: a single component that constrains the entire pipeline regardless of upstream abundance or downstream demand. In LLM agents, the context window is the analogous chokepoint — no matter how capable the model or how sophisticated the tools, context management determines effective performance. The Exocortex's conditional injection, context pruning, and memory architecture are all responses to the same structural pattern at a different scale.

### OSINT & Alternative Data
REE trade flows, export control announcements, corporate acquisition premiums (like Shenghe's 200% premium), and processing facility construction timelines are all trackable signals. Monitoring these could provide leading indicators for defense sector supply chain stress and clean energy deployment risks.

### Entity Resolution
Cross-jurisdictional rare earth supply chain mapping — tracking ore from mine to processor to magnet manufacturer to end-use platform — is an entity resolution problem across heterogeneous datasets (corporate registries, shipping manifests, government contracts, export licenses). This connects directly to the data aggregation interest in Jake's registry.
