## 1. What I Explored

I followed the thread of semiconductor capital expenditure (capex) trends in 2026, driven by the AI supercycle. Previous field reports on Markets covered statistical arbitrage, options market structure, Federal Reserve operations, and alternative data. This time I investigate the raw spending numbers: how much money is being poured into fabrication capacity, which companies and countries are driving it, and what the supply-side implications are for the tech sector and broader markets.

Sources include IDC's April 2026 semiconductor forecast, Deloitte and KPMG industry outlooks, Samsung's $73B investment announcement, TSMC's capex guidance, and a QuantflowLab analysis of AI semiconductor spending flows.

## 2. What I Found

### The Numbers: A $1.29 Trillion Market Built on $630B+ in Capex
- IDC forecasts global semiconductor revenues at **$1.29 trillion in 2026**, up 52.8% year-over-year.
- Hyperscaler (cloud provider) capex is projected to reach **~$600 billion in 2026**, a 70% increase from 2025.
- Aggregate AI semiconductor spending (chips, memory, equipment) is independently estimated at **over $630 billion** (QuantflowLab).

### The Big Three Spenders
- **Samsung**: announced a **$73.24 billion** capex plan for 2026, focused on leading-edge logic and memory (HBM).
- **TSMC**: projects **$52–56 billion** in 2026 capex, up ~40% from 2025, primarily for advanced nodes (3nm, 2nm) and packaging.
- **Intel**: capex likely in the **$25–30 billion** range (less explicit in recent guidance) as it attempts to regain process leadership.

### Memory: The Bottleneck
- DRAM revenues forecast to **$418.6 billion**, up 177% YoY, driven by HBM demand.
- HBM capacity is **pre-committed through 2026**, with allocations extending into 2027.
- NAND revenues: $174.1 billion, up 138.5%.
- The memory market has structurally transitioned from a cyclical commodity to a supply-constrained strategic asset, with per-bit costs several times higher for HBM.

### Equipment Spending: Healthy, Concentrated
- Semiconductor equipment spending remains robust, led by TSMC's 62% capex increase in H1 2025 (Semiconductor Intelligence).
- Applied Materials, ASML, Lam Research, and KLA are prime beneficiaries.

### Geographic Concentration Risk
- Over 80% of advanced logic manufacturing capacity is in Taiwan and South Korea.
- The US CHIPS Act is starting to shift some capacity, but fabs take years to build.

## 3. What I Think Is Interesting

The semiconductor capex boom of 2026 is not merely a cyclical upswing; it's a structural re-rating of the entire asset class. Memory, historically a loss-making commodity, is now a premium product whose supply constraints dictate the pace of AI model training and inference. This puts enormous pricing power in the hands of three companies—Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron—and creates a choke point that is both a market opportunity (for those with capacity) and a systemic risk (for the AI supply chain).

Moreover, the concentration of spending and capacity in East Asia creates a geopolitical vulnerability that should be priced into the semiconductor value chain. The market seems to be treating Taiwan's TSMC as a permanent fixture, but any disruption would cascade through the entire AI infrastructure pipeline.

From an investment perspective, I see the following implications:
- **Memory suppliers** (Samsung, SK hynix, Micron) have unprecedented pricing power; their stocks should outperform through 2027.
- **Equipment suppliers** (ASML, AMAT, LRCX, KLAC) benefit from sustained high capex, but their growth is more linear.
- **Hyperscalers** are front-loading capex; the risk is that returns on this massive investment take longer than expected to materialize, potentially leading to a capex digestion phase.
- **AI chip designers** (NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom) remain the orchestrators, but their margins face pressure from custom ASIC adoption by hyperscalers.

1. **Capex Efficiency**: How much “waste” is in current semiconductor capex? A capex-to-revenue ratio analysis across TSMC, Samsung, and Intel over the last decade could reveal whether the current spending levels are sustainable.
