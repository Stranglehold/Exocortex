## Overview

The US LNG export buildout has transformed American natural gas from a continental fuel into a geopolitical asset. In 2023 the US became the world's largest LNG exporter; by 2026 it operates 9 large-scale terminals with ~17-18 Bcf/d of export capacity, a structural advantage that became strategically critical during the Strait of Hormuz crisis because — unlike crude — most LNG trade does not need to transit the strait. Qatari LNG (the world's largest exporter) must pass Hormuz, giving US Gulf Coast LNG an enduring rerouting advantage.

This page is the dedicated treatment of the buildout as a geopolitical lever and market-structure event, complementing [[energy-commodity-dynamics]] and [[energy-commodity-dynamics-post-hormuz]] which cover the broader oil/gas complex.

### First- and Second-Wave Project Milestones
- **Golden Pass LNG (Texas, 18 mtpa):** first LNG production March 2026, first cargo April 22, 2026 — the 9th US export terminal; startup delayed by contractor bankruptcy (Zachry). EIA Today in Energy 2026-04.
- **Plaquemines LNG (Louisiana):** Phase 1 ramping (10 mtpa), first cargoes late 2024; Phase 2 FID (10 mtpa), startup ~2027. Venture Global.
- **Corpus Christi Stage 3 (Cheniere):** first cargoes late 2024. Adds ~10 mtpa.
- **Rio Grande LNG Phase 1 (Texas, 17.6 mtpa):** FID 2023, startup ~2027-2028; FERC extension May 2026; Phase 2 in second wave.
- **Port Arthur LNG Phase 1 (Texas, 13.5 mtpa):** FID 2023, startup ~2028.
- **CP2 LNG (Louisiana, 20 mtpa):** DOE non-FTA authorization paused 2024; pause lifted early 2025 — moved toward FID/construction, the bellwether of the second wave.
- **Commonwealth LNG (Louisiana):** second-wave FID candidate post-pause-lift.

## Regulatory & Infrastructure Chokepoints
- **DOE non-FTA authorizations:** the binding regulatory constraint. The Biden-era pause (2024) froze CP2; the early-2025 lift unlocked the second wave. Post-Hormuz energy-security politics accelerated the political case for approvals.
- **FERC siting/permitting:** Rio Grande extension (May 2026) shows project-level friction remains.
- **Pipeline takeaway:** ~18 Bcf/d of new export capacity under construction requires new Gulf Coast pipeline capacity; ~44.9 Bcf/d of new pipeline capacity due 2026-2027 (>66% from Texas) supports it but is a scheduling co-dependency.
- **Contractor risk:** Golden Pass delay (Zachry bankruptcy) demonstrated that liquefaction is as much an industrial-construction story as a resource story.

## Price & Arbitrage Mechanics
- Henry Hub remains moderate (~$3.67-4.19/MMBtu range, 2026 expectations) despite global LNG price spikes because export capacity is the binding constraint, not domestic supply.
- TTF (Europe) and JKM (Asia) premiums over Henry Hub create persistent arbitrage; the spread has widened as both basins bid for Gulf Coast cargoes, with elevated shipping costs from increased tonne-mile demand.
- LNG exports are the transmission mechanism linking US domestic gas prices to global geopolitical shocks: Hormuz disruptions raise TTF/JKM, which pulls US cargoes abroad (netting-back to HH through utilization).
- Unlike LNG, Qatari cargoes transiting Hormuz face war-risk insurance, rerouting, and delay premia — the crisis's clearest structural gift to US/Atlantic Basin exporters.

## 2026 Global Supply Wave & Glut Risk
- IEA projects ~345 bcm/yr of new global LNG capacity in 2025-2030.
- The 2026-2027 supply wave (~130 mtpa new liquefaction globally) raises the question of a potential glut and margin compression — the buildout's commercial thesis may peak just as demand growth slows, unless geopolitical disruptions (Hormuz, Russia pipeline politics) preserve premium pricing.
- South Korea is locking in Canadian crude and LNG supply (June 2026); Pakistan is rushing to secure LNG for summer power demand; Australia's Ichthys strike disrupted cargo loadings — demand-side competition remains intense.

## Geopolitical Leverage Framework
1. **Chokepoint arbitrage:** LNG is the energy commodity least exposed to Hormuz; the US is the swing supplier that can redirect cargoes. This converts the buildout into a strategic asset during gray-zone operations.
2. **Alliance binding:** European and Asian buyers contracting long-term with US exporters create commercial interdependence that deepens security relationships (locked-in LNG = structural ally financing).
3. **Sanctions vector:** LNG sales are a licit revenue channel that can be withheld selectively as coercive leverage; conversely, Russian Arctic LNG 2 and future projects face sanctions/insurance exclusion, preserving the US/Qatar duopoly.
4. **Price weapon limits:** unlike crude, gas is regionally priced; US leverage is bounded by liquefaction capacity. Until the second wave completes, export growth is capped by physics, not policy.
