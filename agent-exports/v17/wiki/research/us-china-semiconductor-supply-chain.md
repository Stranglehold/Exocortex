## Overview

The US-China semiconductor supply chain rivalry is the central strategic conflict shaping global technology competition. The semiconductor supply chain represents the most strategically concentrated industrial ecosystem in modern history: Taiwan produces 90%+ of the world's most advanced semiconductors (<7nm), creating a "silicon shield" that is both deterrent and critical vulnerability. The competition has evolved from targeted AI chip restrictions (2022) to comprehensive equipment-based export controls (2023-2025) to entity-based restrictions with built-in calibration mechanisms (2026 MATCH Act), while China pursues aggressive self-sufficiency through domestic equipment maturation and multi-patterning workarounds.

---

## 1. Export Control Architecture (2025-2026)

### 1.1 The MATCH Act (April 2026)

A bipartisan US legislation introduced April 2, 2026 (H.R. 8170), the Multilateral Alignment of Technology Controls on Hardware Act represents the sharpest escalation yet in semiconductor export controls. Key provisions:

- **Entity-based targeting:** Explicitly lists SMIC, Huawei, CXMT (DRAM), Hua Hong/HLMC (foundry), and YMTC (NAND flash) as entities engaged in Military-Civil Fusion. Shifts controls from fab-level to entity-level, closing the loophole where tools sold for trailing-node fabs were redirected to advanced-node production.
- **Extraterritorial enforcement:** Requires allies (Japan, Netherlands, South Korea, Taiwan) to enact equivalent restrictions within 150 days. If coordination fails, foreign-made tools with >0% US technology content or requiring US-origin servicing are subject to unilateral US controls.
- **75% threshold calibration:** Built-in calibration mechanism — if China can domestically meet 75% of demand for a specific tool category (e.g., etching, deposition), US restrictions on that category lift. Acknowledges that American toolmakers lose strategic relevance as Chinese alternatives mature; focuses restrictions where US leverage remains.
- **Circumvention prevention:** Controls attach to end-use, end-user, reexport, and servicing. Third-party routing (Singapore, Malaysia intermediaries) exposes all parties to losing Western fab equipment (WFE) access.
- **Commerce Department waiver revocation (September 2025):** Waivers allowing TSMC, Samsung, SK Hynix to import US-made equipment into their China fabs are being revoked.

### 1.2 Multilateral Coordination

- **Japan:** 23-item equipment list (2023) restricting immersion DUV, etch, deposition, cleaning, inspection tools; expanding under MATCH Act pressure.
- **Netherlands:** ASML EUV exports to China banned since 2019; DUV immersion (NXT:2050i/2100i) restrictions expanded 2023-2026.
- **China's retaliation:** Designation of Japanese companies as controlled entities; gallium and tungsten export restrictions.

## 2. TSMC Arizona — The Supply Chain Backstop

### 2.1 Buildout Status (July 2026)

- **Total US investment:** $165 billion, the largest foreign direct investment in US manufacturing history.
- **Fab 1 (4nm):** Producing. Currently TSMC's most advanced node outside Taiwan.
- **Fab 2 (3nm):** Construction complete; equipment move-in planned Q3 2026 (July-September), high-volume production targeted for 2027.
- **Fab 3 (2nm or below):** Planned.
- **$2B CHIPS Act defense allocation:** Reserved for "trusted foundry" requirements (Aegis, F-35, satellite payloads).

### 2.2 Structural Limitations

- Arizona fabs provide geographical diversification but not organizational redundancy — all advanced capacity remains TSMC-dependent.
- TSMC global foundry revenue: 72% (2025), up from 59% (2020). Samsung at 7%. All leading-edge AI chips (Nvidia, AMD, Apple) depend on a single supplier.
- 2nm ramp: TSMC posted record Q4 2025 driven by 3nm/5nm nodes, with 2nm capacity growth in 2026. Most advanced nodes remain Taiwan-exclusive.

## 3. SMIC & China's Domestic Push

### 3.1 7nm via DUV Multi-Patterning

- SMIC achieved volume production of 7nm-class chips (N+1/N+2) using ASML DUV immersion tools (NXT:1950i/1980Di) with multi-patterning — the exact equipment the MATCH Act now restricts.
- Used in Huawei's Kirin 9030 processor (5nm-class N+3 process), confirmed by TechInsights December 2025.
- Yields only 30-40%, heavily subsidized by Chinese government. Multi-patterning increases cost and cycle time but enables domestic advanced-node production without EUV.

### 3.2 5nm and 3nm Ambitions

- SMIC N+3: Volume production of 5nm-class process for Kirin 9030 (December 2025).
- 3nm GAA (gate-all-around): SMIC and Huawei reportedly targeting 3nm GAA tape-out in 2026 (Business Korea, June 2025). Goal is mass production by 2027.
- China aims to increase 7nm/5nm output fivefold in two years while simultaneously developing domestic EUV laser-driven plasma sources.

### 3.3 Chinese Domestic Equipment Maturation

- **Naura Technology Group:** Etch, deposition, and thermal processing tools; competing with Applied Materials and Lam Research in mature nodes.
- **Advanced Micro-Fabrication Equipment Inc. (AMEC):** Plasma etching and MOCVD tools; expanding into advanced-node applications.
- **Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment (SMEE):** Developing domestic lithography tools (28nm-class, targeting 14nm).
- **National IC Investment Fund (Big Fund III):** $47B+ deployed to accelerate domestic equipment and materials ecosystem.
- The 75% threshold in the MATCH Act implicitly acknowledges that Chinese alternatives are approaching competitive parity in some categories — US restrictions that persist beyond domestic maturation become strategically irrelevant.

- High-NA EUV (EXE:5200) for 2nm and below — only ASML produces these tools.

## 5. Geopolitical Dynamics & Calibration

### 5.1 From Containment to Calibration

The MATCH Act's 75% threshold signals a shift from containment logic (indefinite tightening) to structured strategic calibration. This acknowledges that American toolmakers lose market position as Chinese alternatives mature — controls where leverage exists, relax where it doesn't. Technology restrictions as economic statecraft (Tech Policy Press, April 2026).

### 5.2 Interdependence Complexity

- **EU forced to exempt banned Chinese chipmaker (2026):** After auto industry warned of supply crisis — revealing the interdependence beyond US-China bilateral framework.
- **TSMC capacity reallocation:** Nvidia refocusing TSMC capacity away from China as export controls stall China sales (March 2026).
- **RISC-V implications:** Open-source ISA simplifies chip design but still requires fabrication at advanced nodes — does not resolve the manufacturing chokepoint.

### 5.3 Sanctions Circumvention Patterns

Patterns structurally identical to those observed in Russian oil price cap and Iranian energy smuggling:
- Third-country intermediaries (Singapore, Malaysia) routing restricted equipment.
- Shell company networks obscuring end-user identity.
- MATCH Act's entity-based approach is a direct response to the "audit/verification impossibility" problem that plagues sanctions enforcement.

## 6. Cross-Domain Connections

1. **Defense Sector Consolidation:** Leading-edge chips critical for Aegis, F-35, satellite payloads. CHIPS Act reserves $2B for defense applications. TSMC Arizona directly addresses "trusted foundry" requirements.
2. **Entity Resolution:** MATCH Act's entity-based approach mirrors core entity resolution challenge — resolving corporate identities across jurisdictions, ownership structures, and intermediary chains.
3. **Rare Earth Supply Chains:** Both semiconductor fabrication and rare earth midstream processing share structural chokepoint dynamics — a single geopolitical actor controls a critical industrial node (Taiwan for chips, China for REE processing).
4. **Energy/Markets:** Fabs consume massive electricity (TSMC alone uses ~5% of Taiwan's power). Grid capacity is becoming a fab siting constraint in the US (Arizona water, Ohio power). Semiconductor supply disruption affects every sector depending on advanced chips.
5. **AI Agent Architecture:** TSMC's single-fab concentration is structurally analogous to single-model dependency in AI systems — the need for diversification, redundancy, and graceful degradation.
6. **Supply Chain Economic Warfare:** Export controls are the primary weapon in US-China technology competition. ASML's EUV monopoly and Applied Materials' deposition tools are the chokepoints — structurally identical to energy chokepoint dynamics (Strait of Hormuz, rare earth processing).
7. **FPGA & Hardware:** Xilinx/Intel FPGAs manufactured at TSMC. Capex trends directly affect FPGA availability and pricing for edge AI deployment, custom PCB sensor networks, and TinyML applications.
8. **Bridging Local-Frontier Model Performance:** Hardware availability (H100/B200 vs consumer GPUs) directly constrains local model inference performance. Semiconductor capex trends determine GPU availability and pricing, impacting the local-to-frontier cascade routing architecture.
9. **Sanctions Effectiveness:** Shift from use-based to entity-based controls parallels the evolution from transaction monitoring to entity-level sanctions design — both responses to "audit/verification impossibility."
10. **Intelligence Failure Analysis:** Semiconductor supply chain concentration risk mirrors intelligence failure patterns — the known vulnerability is acknowledged but structural mitigation remains politically difficult, creating conditions for future failure.
