## Overview

Energy commodity markets — crude oil, natural gas, LNG, refined products — are shaped by geopolitical decisions, supply chain chokepoints, and macroeconomic forces. The 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis represents the largest oil supply disruption in history, removing ~20% of global supplies (Dallas Fed, March 2026). This page covers OPEC+ production strategies, US shale breakeven economics, LNG export terminal buildout, strategic petroleum reserves, demand destruction mechanics, supply readjustment patterns, and the US-Iran peace framework fragility recently demonstrated by the July 7, 2026 tanker attacks.

### July 7, 2026 Escalation (BREAKING)

Iran attacked the Qatari LNG tanker **Al-Rekayyat** while it was transiting near the Strait of Hormuz (Qatar MFA spokesperson Dr. Majed al Ansari confirmed). A second tanker was struck by an unidentified projectile on July 7 and believed to have suffered structural damage (UKMTO). The attacks occurred 8 nautical miles east of Limah, Oman.

Critical developments:
- The attacks **reaffirm the fragility** of the US-Iran interim peace agreement (MOU signed June 2026)
- **US revoked Iran's oil sales authorization** after-hours, causing Brent to pop 5.6% to $76.04 and WTI to jump 5.4% to $72.25
- "The MOU in effect with Iran is entirely performance-based" — US official to CNBC
- Berenberg chief economist: "Both sides should ultimately have an interest in containing the conflict. Trump wants low oil prices ahead of November 3 mid-terms whereas the Revolutionary Guards covet the money from potential sanctions relief."

## Dallas Fed Economic Impact Modeling

A Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas study (Kilian, Plante & Richter, March 20, 2026) formally modeled Hormuz closure scenarios using a geopolitical-risk-aware macroeconomic framework.

### Model Assumptions
- **Supply shock magnitude:** ~20% of global oil production removed (3-5× larger than 1973, 1979, 1980, or 1990 disruptions)
- **Probabilistic closure duration:** 100% probability in Q2 2026, declining probability thereafter based on expected duration
- **Key limitation acknowledged:** Model excludes natural gas/fertilizer export disruption effects

### Scenario Outcomes — WTI Oil Price ($/bbl)
| Quarter | Pre-Closure | 1-Quarter Closure | 2-Quarter Closure | 3-Quarter Closure |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Q1 2026 | $60 | $60 | $60 | $60 |
| Q2 2026 | — | $98 | $98 | $98 |
| Q3 2026 | — | $68 | $115 | $115 |
| Q4 2026 | — | $67 | $76 | $132 |

### Scenario Outcomes — Global Real GDP Growth Impact (%)
| Quarter | 1-Quarter Closure | 2-Quarter Closure | 3-Quarter Closure |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Q2 2026 | -2.9 | -2.9 | -2.9 |
| Q3 2026 | +2.2 | -1.2 | -1.2 |
| Q4 2026 | +0.1 | +2.7 | -1.1 |
| Q4/Q4 | -0.2 pp | -0.3 pp | -1.3 pp |

### Key Insights
- Even a 1-quarter closure costs 0.2 pp of global annual GDP growth
- A 3-quarter closure would cause global GDP to end 2026 1.3% below baseline
- **Actual price path has tracked between the 1-quarter and 2-quarter scenario:** WTI hit ~$125 in April (above 2-quarter Q2 model of $98), then declined to ~$70 by July (below 1-quarter Q3 model of $68)
- **Model likely underpredicts peak** due to tanker market dislocations, insurance rate spikes, and expectations dynamics not fully captured

### Mitigation Pathways Identified
1. **Saudi East-West pipeline to Yanbu:** ~4M bpd capacity (one-fifth of global shortfall), but Yanbu within range of Iranian/Houthi missiles
2. **UAE Habshan-Fujairah pipeline:** 1.8M bpd capacity; Mandous storage complex (42M barrels)
3. **Bilateral deals:** China and India negotiating tanker passage agreements with Iran
4. **Tanker War precedent (1980s):** Traffic may resume in hostile environment at lower volumes

## Global Oil Supply Readjustment (IEA Analysis)

### The Scale of Disruption
- **Cumulative oil supply losses from Middle East:** >1.3 billion barrels (IEA, June 22, 2026)
- **Hormuz throughput:** 20M bpd pre-conflict → avg **2.7M bpd** in March-May 2026
- **Pre-war surplus cushion:** 3.7M bpd surplus, 8.2B barrels in storage
- **Global oil inventories falling:** 3.8M bpd drawdown rate since conflict start

### IEA Emergency Response
- **Largest-ever emergency stock release:** 400 million barrels
- **Release rate:** 2.5M bpd of additional oil brought to market in May 2026
- Asia-Pacific members moved first to address regional strain

### Supply-Side Adaptations

| UAE | 4.9M | 4.3M (June, ~85%) | Habshan-Fujairah pipeline + dark transit |

| Kazakhstan | — | Increased | Atlantic Basin rerouting |

### Demand Destruction
- **Global oil demand dropped ~5M bpd in Q2 2026 YoY** (IEA Oil Market Report)
- Pre-war forecast was for +850K bpd demand growth
- Full-year 2026 demand now forecast to **decline by 1.1M bpd**
- **China** slashed crude imports by 40% (4.6M bpd) between February and May

### Refining System Adaptations
- **Jet fuel:** US flipped from net importer (Apr 2025) to net exporter (early 2026); European jet fuel yields hit records; Nigeria's Dangote refinery doubled West African exports
- **Middle East refined product exports collapsed** — refiners in Europe, Nigeria, and US stepped in

## OPEC+ Production Dynamics (2026)

### Strait of Hormuz Crisis Impact

Despite the near-total closure of Hormuz, OPEC+ announced five consecutive monthly production increases of 188,000 bpd starting from July 2026. The seven-country voluntary adjustment mechanism (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, Oman) approved the fifth increase for August 2026.

Key tensions:
- **Production increases largely undeliverable** due to physical inability to export through Hormuz
- **Saudi Arabia compensates** via Red Sea Yanbu terminal (5M bpd, but exposed to Houthi/Iranian missile threat)
- **Iraq and Kuwait** were forced to shut in wells as local storage filled (early March 2026)
- **OPEC+ quota mechanism** operates on theoretical production, not actual export capacity — creating a fiction that may distort market expectations

The pre-war oil surplus (3.7M bpd, driven partly by OPEC+ overproduction and Chinese stockpiling) provided a crucial buffer that prevented even more extreme price spikes.

### Price Impact Channels
- Higher global crude prices pushed diesel and jet fuel wholesale prices up >60% in early 2026 (EIA STEO)
- Supply-demand mismatch exacerbated by simultaneous Hormuz disruption and OPEC+ quota increases
- Physical delivery impossibility creates extreme backwardation risk in futures curves
- The MOU peace framework creates a binary risk structure: normalization premium vs. collapse risk

## US Shale Breakeven Economics

The US shale sector's response to the Hormuz crisis has been fundamentally different from historical oil shocks — characterized by capital discipline rather than aggressive production growth.

### Key Metrics (Q1 2026, Dallas Fed Survey)
| Basin | Average Breakeven ($/bbl) | Large Firm Breakeven ($/bbl) |
|-------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| Permian — Midland | $61 | — |
| Permian — Delaware | $62 | — |
| All Basins (avg) | $67 | $59 |

### Structural Factors
- **2024-2025 M&A consolidation wave:** $250B+ in transactions concentrated the sector
- **DUC inventory depletion:** 1,259 (Q1 2025) → 800 (Q1 2026), limiting rapid production response
- **E&P spending declined:** -4.2% in 2025-2026, reflecting investor pressure for capital returns over growth
- **Permian production plateau:** ~6.1M bpd, Eagle Ford and Bakken in decline
- **Six structural growth constraints:** DUC depletion, labor shortages, frac fleet limitations, pipeline bottlenecks, regulatory uncertainty, investor capital discipline

### Crisis Response Paradox
Despite WTI prices reaching $125/bbl in April 2026 — well above even the highest-cost breakeven — US producers have been slow to increase output. This reflects:
1. **Capital discipline:** Wall Street's insistence on shareholder returns over volume growth
2. **Lead time:** 6-12 months to bring new wells online after increasing rig count
3. **DUC as buffer:** Producers drawing down drilled-but-uncompleted wells to boost short-term production without increasing rig count
4. **Supply chain constraints:** Frac sand, steel casing, and skilled labor bottlenecks

## Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR)

### Status (as of June 25, 2026)
- The SPR has been a critical tool during the Hormuz crisis
- **IEA collective release:** 400M barrels (largest in history)
- **Exchange program mechanism:** Loans of crude to refiners with 18-24% premium repayment in barrels
- The exchange program creates a temporary supply boost while building future SPR inventory at a premium

### Drawdown Mechanics
- **Direct sale:** DOE sells SPR crude via competitive auction; fastest delivery but depletes reserve permanently
- **Exchange:** DOE loans crude to refiners; refiners repay with additional barrels later; avoids permanent depletion
- **Collective action:** IEA-coordinated release among member countries; spreads burden and maximizes market impact
