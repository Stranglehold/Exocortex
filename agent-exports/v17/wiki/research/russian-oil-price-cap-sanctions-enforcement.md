## Overview

The G7/EU price cap on Russian seaborne crude oil ($60/bbl crude, December 2022; $100/$45 products cap, February 2023) represents the most significant coordinated sanctions regime against a major oil exporter in modern history. The cap bans Western maritime services — shipping, insurance, finance, port services — for Russian oil sold above the ceiling. By mid-2026, the policy enters its fourth year of implementation against an increasingly sophisticated evasion ecosystem.

### Mechanism
- Western service providers (insurers, shipowners, financiers) may only facilitate Russian oil shipments if the cargo is sold at or below the cap
- Attestation-based compliance: buyers/sellers provide documentation certifying purchase price compliance
- Maritime services ban for non-compliant cargoes: no P&I insurance, no EU/G7-flagged vessels, no European port calls

## Shadow Fleet: Composition & Growth

The shadow fleet (also called the "ghost fleet" or "dark fleet") is a parallel logistics ecosystem of tankers operating outside Western maritime services to transport sanctioned oil.

### Scale
- **End 2022:** ~600 vessels, ~400 crude tankers
- **Dec 2023:** 1,100–1,400 vessels (estimates)
- **Feb 2026:** 1,337 vessels cataloged by Ukrainian intelligence
- **Total 2026:** Passed 1,300 vessels — ~20% of global oil tanker capacity
- **Combined carrying capacity:** >200 million deadweight tonnes (Lloyd's List Intelligence, US Treasury)
- **Daily throughput:** ~3.7 million barrels/day — 65% of Russian seaborne oil trade (CSIS Dec 2025)

### Vessel Characteristics
- **Age:** 72% are >15 years old, requiring constant maintenance
- **Insurance:** Typically lack proper P&I coverage; rely on opaque non-Western providers
- **Registration:** Flag-of-convenience states; frequent flag-hopping
- **Ownership:** Shell companies in UAE, Hong Kong, Marshall Islands, Seychelles
- **April 2026 data (KSE):** 129 unique tankers used for crude exports (80% shadow fleet); 219 unique tankers for oil products

## Evasion Methods: A Taxonomy

### 1. Ship-to-Ship (STS) Transfers
Cargo is moved between vessels at sea, severing traceable custody chains. Common anchorages: off Port Said (Egypt), Malta, Ceuta, Atlantic approaches.
- **Example:** EU-sanctioned *Mikati* transferred cargo to sanctioned *Noctis* off Port Said (Aug 2025); cargo continued to Ningbo, China
- Combined with AIS manipulation to conceal rendezvous

### 2. AIS Manipulation (Spoofing)
- **"Going dark":** Disabling AIS transponders during port calls at sanctioned loading terminals
- **Position falsification:** Broadcasting false GPS coordinates while actually loading at Russian ports
- Requires satellite imagery (optical/SAR) for real-time detection

### 3. Insurance Attestation Fraud
- Shell-company trading entities produce fraudulent attestation documents understating purchase price
- Western insurers/reinsurers rely solely on attestations from opaque intermediaries registered in UAE, Hong Kong
- No direct access to actual payment flows by maritime service providers
- G7-compliant certificates issued for non-compliant cargoes

### 4. Port Rotation Manipulation
- Vessels obscure loading port identities through AIS gaps and false paperwork
- Multi-port itineraries designed to obfuscate Russian origin

### 5. Flag-Hopping & Corporate Obfuscation
- Frequent changes of vessel flag, name, and registered owner
- Ownership layered through multi-jurisdictional shell structures

## Enforcement by Jurisdiction

### European Union
- **Designated vessels:** ~600 shadow fleet vessels as of early 2026 (~two-thirds of fleet)
- **20th Package (Apr 2026):** 120 new designations (37 individuals, 83 entities), highest in two years; full maritime services ban on Russian crude proposed (implementation pending G7 coordination); financial circumvention measures targeting third-country enablers
- **Effectiveness:** EU/UK sanctions reduce sanctioned vessel productivity by ~30% (Kpler)

### United States
- **Designated vessels:** ~40% of fleet via OFAC; no new vessel designations since January 2025
- **Operation Southern Spear:** Naval blockade of sanctioned tankers near Venezuela; at least 10 tankers seized since Dec 2025, including Russian-flagged *Marinera* (North Atlantic, Jan 2026)
- **Effectiveness:** OFAC restrictions reduce sanctioned vessel productivity by ~70% — approximately twice as effective as EU/UK measures (Kpler)

### United Kingdom
- Announced military boarding authority for shadow fleet vessels in UK waters (Mar 2026)
- No detentions reported as of April 2026
- Dozens of sanctioned vessels transited English Channel in early 2026 (BBC Verify, Jan 2026)

### Other Notable Actions
- **Belgium:** Special forces seized false-flagged tanker *Ethera* in North Sea (Feb 2026)
- **Sweden:** Brief detention of *Flora 1* after 12-km oil slick in Baltic Sea (Apr 2026)

## Environmental & Safety Risks

The shadow fleet's operational profile creates severe environmental hazards:
- **Aging fleet:** 72% of vessels >15 years old, requiring constant maintenance
- **No classification society oversight:** Vessels operate without proper surveys or inspections
- **Inadequate insurance:** Dubious or nonexistent P&I coverage; coastal states bear cleanup costs
- **Single-spill cost:** Could exceed €1 billion for coastal taxpayers
- **Incident example:** *Flora 1* 12-kilometer oil slick, Baltic Sea (Apr 2026); legal proof of responsibility elusive

## Effectiveness Assessment

### Enforcement Divergence
- **OFAC sanctions** twice as effective as EU/UK (70% vs 30% productivity reduction, Kpler)
- **US pause** on new designations since Jan 2025 creates enforcement gap
- **Shadow fleet share** of exports rising: reached 54% in April 2026 — highest on record (CREA)
- **Revenue resilience:** Iran conflict price spike in March 2026 doubled monthly revenue despite sanctions

### Structural Challenges
- Attestation-based system inherently vulnerable to fraud
- Limited Western visibility into actual payment flows
- Shell-company opacity in UAE, Hong Kong jurisdictional havens
- Tanker-supply constraints drive demand for shadow vessels when legitimate capacity is tight
